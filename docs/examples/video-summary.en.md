## About
A detailed explanation of how the measles virus bypasses the immune system, its physiological effects, and the importance of vaccination for herd immunity.

## Key ideas and facts
- Measles is a highly contagious virus that requires a host cell to reproduce and primarily enters through the nose, mouth, or eyes.
- The virus targets the first line of defense, macrophages in the lungs, turning them into virus production centers.
- It uses dendritic cells as a 'Trojan horse' to travel to lymph nodes, where it then infects T and B cells.
- Once in the bloodstream, it spreads to the spleen, liver, intestines, and lungs, causing high fever, bronchitis, and a characteristic rash.
- The virus wipes out lung defenses, making the body susceptible to secondary bacterial infections like pneumonia, which is the leading cause of measles-related deaths.
- In severe cases, the virus can infect the brain, carrying a 20-40% mortality rate and potential for permanent damage.
- Recovery takes 2-3 weeks, but the immune system remains severely weakened for months afterward.
- Measles is so contagious that an unvaccinated person has a 90% chance of infection if exposed.
- Vaccination is essential for 'herd immunity' to protect those who cannot be vaccinated, such as infants, chemotherapy patients, or those with HIV.
- In 2014 alone, despite 84% global vaccination coverage, 122,000 people died from measles.

## What is new / practically useful
- Explanation of the 'Trojan horse' mechanism involving dendritic cells.
- The fact that pneumonia, not the virus itself, is the most common cause of death due to compromised lung immunity.
- Clarification that measles does not 'strengthen' the immune system but actually weakens it for months post-infection.
- Statistical risk of infection (90%) upon exposure for the unvaccinated.
